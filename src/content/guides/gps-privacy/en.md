When you take a photo with a smartphone, the image can quietly record where you were standing. This guide explains how GPS location metadata gets into your photos, what the risk really is, and how to strip it out before you share.

## How does GPS get into a photo?

Cameras with built-in GPS, or phones that use their location services, can write your position — typically latitude and longitude — directly into the file's metadata. Many phones do this by default when location is enabled, so the coordinates end up in the JPEG with no extra step from you.

The stored position is usually precise to a small area, often the very spot where the photo was taken. Because it is embedded in the file, it travels with the image anywhere you send it.

## What can someone learn from your location data?

A set of coordinates can reveal a surprising amount:

- **Where you live** — a photo taken at home can point straight to your address.
- **Where you work and travel** — timestamps plus locations build a picture of your routine.
- **Where your children are** — photos taken at school or at home can expose sensitive details.
- **Your habits** — regular shots at a gym, cafe, or clinic are easy to piece together.

This is why location metadata is one of the first things to clean before publishing photos online.

## Are platforms safe to rely on?

No. Many social networks and messaging apps strip some metadata when you upload, but behaviour varies by platform and format, and it can change without notice. Some services re-compress images in ways that preserve or even add data. The reliable habit is to remove location metadata yourself, in your own workflow, before you share, rather than trusting a third party to do it.

## Localisation and file formats

GPS data is not unique to JPEG. Many formats — including HEIC, PNG, and even scans saved with certain tools — can carry location or other metadata. Whatever the format, the same principle applies: strip the metadata you do not want before you share.

## The bottom line

Your photos can act as a detailed map of your life, and GPS metadata is the most personal part of that record. Removing it before you share is quick, free, and a real improvement to your privacy posture — even though no single tool can guarantee complete anonymity online.
